## Ankle Sprain Recovery Principles

After a mild-to-moderate ankle sprain, early protected movement (as pain allows)
generally leads to better outcomes than prolonged immobilization. A typical progression
moves from pain-free range-of-motion work (ankle circles, alphabet tracing) in the first
days, to weight-bearing balance work (single-leg stands, progressing to unstable
surfaces) once walking is comfortable, to sport-specific loading (jogging, then cutting
and jumping) only once single-leg balance and full pain-free range of motion are
restored. Returning to running before balance and range of motion are restored is one of
the most common causes of repeat sprains.

## General Mobility Principles

Mobility work is most valuable when targeted at the ranges of motion a person's training
actually demands and currently lacks, rather than applied generically - a lifter with
poor ankle dorsiflexion benefits far more from targeted calf/ankle mobility work before
squatting than from an unrelated general stretching routine. Consistency (a few minutes,
most days) beats occasional long sessions for improving range of motion over time.

## Rehabilitation Loading Principles

Effective rehab progressively increases tissue tolerance to load rather than simply
waiting for pain to disappear before resuming activity - some discomfort during
rehab exercises (typically kept low, e.g. 3/10) is often acceptable and doesn't indicate
harm, provided it settles quickly afterward and isn't worsening session to session.
Complete rest tends to deondition tissue and can lengthen recovery time compared to
appropriately dosed loading.

## Return-to-Sport Guidelines

A safe return to sport after injury should be criteria-based, not calendar-based -
progressing based on demonstrated capacity (e.g. pain-free single-leg hop, comparable
strength to the uninjured side) rather than a fixed number of weeks, since recovery
timelines vary significantly between individuals for the same injury type. A staged
return (non-contact training, then limited contact, then full training, then
competition) with no setback at each stage before progressing further reduces re-injury
risk substantially compared to jumping straight back into full competition.

## Load Management to Prevent Injury

Sharp week-to-week increases in training load (especially high-speed running volume or
total session count) are a well-established injury risk factor, independent of absolute
fitness level. Keeping week-to-week increases in total load moderate, and being
particularly cautious after a period of reduced training (illness, holiday, injury
recovery), meaningfully reduces soft-tissue injury risk.
